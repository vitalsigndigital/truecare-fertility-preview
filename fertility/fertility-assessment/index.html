Fertility · Assessment

Fertility assessment and testing

A fertility assessment at True Care looks at the main factors that affect conception — ovulation and ovarian reserve, the uterus and fallopian tubes, sperm, and overall health — using bloodwork, ultrasound and semen analysis, much of it done on site in Hamilton or Mississauga. It gives you and your physician a clear picture before any treatment is chosen.

When is it time for a fertility assessment?

Infertility is usually defined as not conceiving after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse — or after 6 months if you are 35 or older. It is common: about 1 in 6 people worldwide are affected at some point in their lives (World Health Organization, 2023).

It makes sense to be assessed sooner if you have:

  • Irregular or absent periods, or a diagnosis of PCOS
  • Known or suspected endometriosis, or previous pelvic surgery or infection
  • Two or more pregnancy losses
  • A partner with a known sperm problem
  • Plans to use donor sperm or donor eggs
  • Upcoming treatment, such as chemotherapy, that may affect fertility

What does a fertility assessment include?

Your physician chooses tests based on your history. A typical female assessment draws on the following:

Transvaginal ultrasound
Baseline and mid-cycle imaging of the uterus and ovaries
Antral follicle count (AFC)
Ultrasound count of resting follicles — a marker of ovarian reserve
Ovarian reserve bloodwork
AMH, and day-3 FSH, LH and estradiol
Thyroid and prolactin
Hormones that can disrupt ovulation
Androgen profile
Hormonal and metabolic assessment, including for PCOS
Metabolic labs
A1c, fasting insulin and lipid panel
Infectious disease screening
HIV, hepatitis B and C, syphilis, and STI screening
Genetic carrier screening
Testing for inherited conditions
Saline infusion sonohysterography
Ultrasound with saline to outline the uterine cavity
Endometrial biopsy
Tissue sampling, including CD138 staining for chronic inflammation
Hysteroscopy
A camera view of the uterine cavity, diagnostic or operative
Pap test
Cervical screening
MRI pelvis
Advanced imaging, ordered externally when needed

Because a male factor is involved in many couples’ infertility, the partner’s assessment usually runs in parallel — see male fertility testing.

What about recurrent pregnancy loss?

After two or more pregnancy losses, a focused workup looks for treatable causes. It can include an antiphospholipid antibody panel, a thrombophilia panel, parental karyotypes, thyroid and metabolic testing, and imaging of the uterine cavity by sonohysterography or hysteroscopy.

In many cases of recurrent loss no single cause is found. That is frustrating, but it is not the end of the conversation — your physician will explain what the results mean for a future pregnancy.

What happens after the tests?

You review the results with your physician — in person or by video — and talk through the options that fit. Depending on what the tests show, the plan might be timed intercourse with cycle monitoring, ovulation induction and IUI, IVF, treatment of an underlying condition such as PCOS or endometriosis, or fertility preservation.

Questions

Common questions

Does my AMH level tell me whether I can get pregnant?

Not on its own. AMH reflects the number of eggs remaining (ovarian reserve), not their quality, and it does not predict natural conception by itself. It is most useful alongside age, ultrasound and your history — for example, to plan medication doses for IVF or to inform egg-freezing decisions.

Do both partners need to be tested?

Where there is a partner providing sperm, yes. Testing both partners at the start avoids months of treatment aimed at the wrong problem.

Is a sonohysterogram painful?

Most people feel cramping, similar to a period, during and shortly after the procedure. Your care team will explain how to prepare and whether to take a pain reliever beforehand.

Can I do some of my appointments by video?

Yes. Consultations and results reviews can often be done by video. Bloodwork and ultrasound happen at the clinic, often on the same day as an in-person visit.
